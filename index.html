THE STORY BEHIND THE SCALE

A higher sea. A human perspective.

This educational app makes large changes in sea level easier to picture. It compares an estimated ground height with a chosen sea level; it does not predict flooding at your address.

How to use the app
  1. Choose a place. Enter an address, or zoom into the globe and tap the ground. Check that the selected place is the one you intended.
  2. Choose a sea level. Move the slider between 0 and +70 m. The starting scene is an example until you select a location.
  3. Read the comparison. Below the selected sea level, the ruler measures water above the ground. Above it, the person stands on land and the ruler measures down to the sea.

The person is exactly 2 m tall as a scale reference, not an average adult. At 6 m of depth, three person heights fit between the ground and the measurement line. Ripples and sun rays are decorative; pause them anytime.

To share a selected location and sea-level setting, copy the page address. On a keyboard, focus the globe, explore with arrow keys, zoom with + / −, and press Enter to measure its centre.

Why +70 m? The two great ice sheets

If all Earth’s glaciers and ice sheets melted, USGS gives an approximate global sea-level rise of 70 m (about 230 ft). This is the app’s rounded all-land-ice-melt scenario, with no prediction date. It means a higher global average sea level, not 70 m of water above every location. Source: USGS ↗

Earth’s two major ice sheets hold most of its land ice. NSIDC estimates these contributions if each melted completely:

These estimates total about 65.4 m. Other glaciers and ice caps contribute a smaller amount, but they do not explain the entire difference from 70 m. Published estimates use different assumptions and rounding; the two figures are not an exact breakdown of the app’s +70 m setting.

How we calculate your result

We sample the ground at your selected coordinates using the most detailed terrain available from Cesium World Terrain. Its heights are measured from a mathematical Earth surface called the WGS84 ellipsoid. We correct that height using an EGM96 geoid model, which approximates mean sea level, so ground and water use a consistent reference.

Local difference = selected sea-level rise − estimated ground elevation

For example: ground at 64 m and a +70 m sea gives 70 − 64 = 6 m above the ground. Divide by the 2 m reference person: 6 ÷ 2 = 3 person heights. Ground at 100 m instead sits 30 m above that same sea.

The person, ruler and measurement line share one scale. The calculation keeps its full precision; displayed labels are rounded. The cliff is a schematic cross-section, not a reconstruction of the actual site.

The height correction, in detail

Let h be the terrain’s ellipsoid height and N the local geoid offset. Ground elevation is H = h − N. For selected rise R, we draw the local map water at ellipsoid height R + N, and compute the signed difference as R − H.

The bundled GeographicLib-format EGM96 grid has 15-arc-minute spacing. Our code uses bilinear interpolation between neighbouring grid values. This corrects the height reference; it does not turn the terrain into a precise site survey. If terrain or geoid data is unavailable, we withhold the computed local depth.

How accurate is it?

The drawing has a consistent scale. The real-world result is an estimate. Terrain resolution and source quality vary by location. An address may resolve to an area rather than an exact building, and the geoid model also has uncertainty. Displayed decimal places do not imply survey-level accuracy; we cannot give one reliable error margin for every place.

This is a ground-height comparison. It does not test whether ocean water could reach a point through surrounding terrain, or model coastal barriers, drainage, tides, storm surge, erosion or future changes in the ground. The map water is a local illustration, not a computed flood boundary.

Real sea-level change would also vary around the world as gravity, land height and ocean conditions change. The app applies the selected rise uniformly relative to its sea-level reference. It does not simulate those regional effects or warming-related expansion of seawater. Learn more: NASA sea-level science ↗

Use this for exploration and education, not property, insurance or evacuation decisions.
